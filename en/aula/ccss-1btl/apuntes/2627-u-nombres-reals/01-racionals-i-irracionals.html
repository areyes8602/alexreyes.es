1. Rational and irrational numbers

The two sets that make up the real numbers, and how to tell them apart by their decimal expansion.

Rational numbers

Definition

These are all the numbers that can be written as a fraction:

$$\mathbb{Q} = \left\{\, \dfrac{a}{b} \;\mid\; a,\, b \in \mathbb{Z},\; b \neq 0 \,\right\}$$

Example

$$\dfrac{1}{3}, \qquad \dfrac{1}{2}, \qquad 5.\overline{3} = \dfrac{48}{9}, \qquad 1.0\overline{52} = \dfrac{521}{495}, \qquad 1.3 = \dfrac{13}{10}$$

The bar over the digits means that those digits repeat for ever. Every terminating or repeating decimal can be written as a fraction, and so it is rational.

Irrational numbers

Definition

$\mathbb{I}$ is the set of numbers that cannot be written as a fraction.

Example

$$\pi, \qquad e, \qquad \sqrt{2}, \qquad 1.23456789101112\ldots, \qquad 1.010010001\ldots$$

The decimal expansion of an irrational number is infinite and non-repeating: the digits never settle into a repeating block. The last two examples are built on purpose to show this.

Two irrationals with a name

$\pi \approx 3.14159\ldots$ is the ratio of a circle’s circumference to its diameter.

$e \approx 2.71828\ldots$ is the base of the natural logarithms.

The real numbers

The real numbers ($\mathbb{R}$) are the rationals together with the irrationals.

  • $\mathbb{N}$, the naturals: $0,\, 1,\, 2,\, 3,\ \ldots$
  • $\mathbb{Z}$, the integers: $\ldots,\, -2,\, -1,\, 0,\, 1,\, 2,\ \ldots$
  • $\mathbb{Q}$, the rationals: those that can be written as a fraction.
  • $\mathbb{I}$, the irrationals: those that cannot.
  • $\mathbb{R}$, the reals: the rationals and the irrationals together.
