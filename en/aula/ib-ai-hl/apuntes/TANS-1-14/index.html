Matrices: definitions and types

What a matrix is, how its entries are named, and the types that will come up throughout the unit.

What a matrix is

Matrix

A matrix is a mathematical object that arranges numbers in rows and columns. The numbers can come from any of the familiar sets: $\mathbb{N}$, $\mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbb{Q}$ or $\mathbb{R}$.

Order

The order of a matrix is its number of rows and columns. We write $A_{n\times m}$ for the matrix $A$ with $n$ rows and $m$ columns: rows first, then columns.

Example

$$A_{2\times 3} = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 1 & 4 \\ 2 & 3 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$$

A matrix is a way of storing data in order. In class the rows were labelled A and B and the columns M, F and SoS:

MFSoS
A514
B231

The entries

$a_{i,j}$ (or $a_{ij}$) is the entry in row $i$ and column $j$. In the example, $a_{1,2} = 1$ and $a_{2,3} = 1$. A matrix is sometimes written in short as $A = (a_{ij})$.

Types of matrix

Zero matrix

All its entries are $0$. It can have any order:

$$O_{3\times 2} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$

Square matrix

It has as many rows as columns. An $n\times n$ matrix is said to have order $n$:

$$A_{3\times 3} = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 & 3 \\ 0 & -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} \quad\text{has order } 3.$$

In a square matrix, the entries $a_{11}, a_{22}, \dots, a_{nn}$ form the leading diagonal; here, $5$, $1$ and $2$.

Diagonal matrix

A square matrix whose entries off the leading diagonal are all $0$: $a_{ij} = 0$ for all $i \neq j$.

$$A_{2\times 2} = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$$

Correction to the board

In class, $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, of order $2\times 3$, came up as an example of a diagonal matrix. It satisfies $a_{ij} = 0$ for $i \neq j$, but it is not square, and diagonal matrices (like the identity and triangular matrices) are only defined for square matrices.

Identity matrix

The square diagonal matrix with every entry on the diagonal equal to $1$. It is written $I$, or $I_n$ when the order needs to be shown:

$$I_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \qquad\qquad I_3 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$$

Triangular matrices

Square matrices with zeros on one side of the leading diagonal.

Upper triangular: every entry below the diagonal is $0$, that is, $a_{ij} = 0$ if $i > j$.

$$\begin{pmatrix} 5 & 1 & 2 \\ \textcolor{#be123c}{0} & 1 & 3 \\ \textcolor{#be123c}{0} & \textcolor{#be123c}{0} & 2 \end{pmatrix}$$

Lower triangular: every entry above the diagonal is $0$, that is, $a_{ij} = 0$ if $i < j$.

$$\begin{pmatrix} 5 & \textcolor{#be123c}{0} & \textcolor{#be123c}{0} \\ 1 & 1 & \textcolor{#be123c}{0} \\ 2 & 3 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$$

A diagonal matrix is both upper and lower triangular.

Correction to the board

The example of an upper triangular matrix in class had order $3\times 4$:

$$\begin{pmatrix} 5 & 1 & 2 & 0 \\ \textcolor{#be123c}{0} & 1 & 3 & 2 \\ \textcolor{#be123c}{0} & \textcolor{#be123c}{0} & 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$$

It has zeros below the diagonal, but it is not square, so it is not triangular. A matrix like this is in row echelon form, and it will come back when solving systems of equations with matrices.

Homework · Example 2: powers of a matrix and Fibonacci

Question

Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$. Find $A^{2}$, $A^{3}$, $A^{4}$ and $A^{5}$. Which numbers appear? Write down an expression for $A^{n}$.

The powers, one after another

Each power comes from multiplying the previous one by $A$: $A^{k+1} = A^{k}\cdot A$.

$$A^{2} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$$
$$A^{3} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$$
$$A^{4} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 & 3 \\ 1 & 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 3 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$$
$$A^{5} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 & 3 \\ 1 & 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 3 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 5 & 5 \\ 2 & 3 & 3 \\ 3 & 5 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$$

The pattern

The entries are Fibonacci numbers, $F_1 = 1,\ F_2 = 1,\ F_3 = 2,\ F_4 = 3,\ F_5 = 5,\ F_6 = 8, \dots$, where each term is the sum of the two before it. With $F_0 = 0$, for $n \ge 2$:

$$A^{n} = \begin{pmatrix} F_{n-1} & F_n & F_n \\ F_{n-2} & F_{n-1} & F_{n-1} \\ F_{n-1} & F_n & F_n \end{pmatrix}$$

Check with $A^{5}$: $F_4 = 3$, $F_5 = 5$ and $F_3 = 2$.

And not only the entries: in $A^{5}$ the rows add up to $13$, $8$ and $13$, the columns to $8$, $13$ and $13$, and the whole matrix to $34$. In general, the rows add up to $F_{n+2}$, $F_{n+1}$ and $F_{n+2}$, and the total is $F_{n+4}$.

Why Fibonacci appears

Multiplying a row of $A^{n}$ by $A$, the product does exactly the Fibonacci rule, adding the previous two:

$$\left(F_{n-1},\ F_n,\ F_n\right)\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \left(F_n,\ F_{n-1}+F_n,\ F_{n-1}+F_n\right) = \left(F_n,\ F_{n+1},\ F_{n+1}\right)$$

That is the first row of $A^{n+1}$, and the second row works the same way one place further back. Since the pattern holds for $n = 2$, it holds for every power: it is a proof by induction.

For anyone who wants to go further

$A$ can be read as the map of a directed graph with three nodes: $a_{ij} = 1$ if there is an arrow from node $i$ to node $j$. Then entry $(i,j)$ of $A^{n}$ counts the paths of $n$ steps from $i$ to $j$, and the sum of the whole matrix, $F_{n+4}$, counts all the paths of $n$ steps.

In my paper A Fibonacci theorem for Collatz trajectories via modular graph structure (arXiv:2606.02621) the Fibonacci numbers turn up the same way: by counting paths in a directed graph, the graph of the transitions of the Collatz conjecture modulo $6$.
