Polar and exponential form

The other two ways of writing a complex number, and why they make multiplying, dividing and raising to powers almost calculation-free. And at the end, what they are for with waves: adding two sinusoids with the same frequency.

From Cartesian to polar form

A complex number is fixed by its real and imaginary parts, but also by how far it is from the origin and at what angle: the modulus and argument from the previous section.

Polar (modulus–argument) form

If $r = |z|$ and $\theta = \arg(z)$, then $a = r\cos\theta$ and $b = r\sin\theta$, so

$$z = a + b\,i = r\left(\cos\theta + i\,\sin\theta\right)$$

This is shortened to $z = r\operatorname{cis}\theta$. In class we write it $z = r_{\theta}$: the modulus first, with the argument as a subscript.

Exponential (Euler) form

$$e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\,\sin\theta \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad z = r\,e^{i\theta}$$

The first equality is Euler's formula. With $\theta = \pi$ it gives Euler's identity,

$$e^{i\pi} + 1 = 0$$

which brings together $e$, $i$, $\pi$, $1$ and $0$ in five symbols.

The three forms are the same number written three ways. Cartesian form is good for adding and subtracting; polar and exponential form, for multiplying, dividing and raising to powers.

Multiplying, dividing and raising to powers

The three rules

Moduli are multiplied or divided; arguments are added or subtracted.

$$r_{\theta} \cdot s_{\varphi} = (r\,s)_{\theta+\varphi} \qquad \dfrac{r_{\theta}}{s_{\varphi}} = \left(\dfrac{r}{s}\right)_{\theta-\varphi} \qquad \left(r_{\theta}\right)^{n} = \left(r^{n}\right)_{n\theta}$$

In exponential form there is nothing to memorise: they are the usual laws of exponents.

$$r\,e^{i\theta} \cdot s\,e^{i\varphi} = r\,s\;e^{i(\theta+\varphi)}$$

Watch out for full turns

The argument is only defined up to whole turns: $\theta$ and $\theta + 2\pi$ are the same angle. After adding or multiplying arguments, it is worth bringing the result back into $[0,\, 2\pi)$ by adding or subtracting $2\pi$.

Exercise 8F.1

Question

a) Given $a = 1+\sqrt{3}\,i$, $\;b = -1+i\;$ and $\;c = \sqrt{3}-i$, write them in modulus–argument form using $\tan\frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}$, $\tan\frac{\pi}{4} = 1$ and $\tan\frac{\pi}{3} = \sqrt{3}$.

b) Hence find, in Cartesian form,  i) $ac$,  ii) $\dfrac{a}{c}$,  iii) $b^{4}$,  iv) $\dfrac{a^{3}}{b^{2}}$  and  v) $\dfrac{a^{2}b}{c^{2}}$.

a) All three in polar form

NumberModulusArgumentPolar form
$a = 1+\sqrt{3}\,i$ $\sqrt{1+3} = 2$ $\arctan\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{1} = \dfrac{\pi}{3}$ $2_{\pi/3}$
$b = -1+i$ $\sqrt{1+1} = \sqrt{2}$ $\arctan\dfrac{1}{-1} = -\dfrac{\pi}{4} \;\to\; +\pi = \dfrac{3\pi}{4}$ $\sqrt{2}_{\,3\pi/4}$
$c = \sqrt{3}-i$ $\sqrt{3+1} = 2$ $\arctan\dfrac{-1}{\sqrt{3}} = -\dfrac{\pi}{6} \;\to\; +2\pi = \dfrac{11\pi}{6}$ $2_{\,11\pi/6}$

$a$ is in the first quadrant and the inverse tangent already gives the right angle. $b$ is in the second, so $\pi$ has to be added; $c$ is in the fourth, and $2\pi$ is added only to make it positive.

b.i) $ac$

Moduli times moduli, arguments plus arguments:

$$ac = 2_{\pi/3} \cdot 2_{\,11\pi/6} = 4_{\,\pi/3 + 11\pi/6} = 4_{\,13\pi/6} = 4_{\,\pi/6}$$

And back to Cartesian form with cosine and sine:

$$ac = 4\left(\cos\dfrac{\pi}{6} + i\,\sin\dfrac{\pi}{6}\right) = 4\left(\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2} + \dfrac{1}{2}\,i\right) = 2\sqrt{3} + 2i$$

Check in Cartesian form: $(1+\sqrt{3}i)(\sqrt{3}-i) = \sqrt{3} - i + 3i - \sqrt{3}\,i^{2} = 2\sqrt{3} + 2i$.

b.ii) $\dfrac{a}{c}$

$$\dfrac{a}{c} = \dfrac{2_{\pi/3}}{2_{\,11\pi/6}} = 1_{\,\pi/3 - 11\pi/6} = 1_{-3\pi/2} = 1_{\,\pi/2} = i$$

$-\frac{3\pi}{2}$ and $\frac{\pi}{2}$ are the same angle: they differ by one turn. And the complex number with modulus $1$ and argument $\frac{\pi}{2}$ is exactly $i$.

b.iii) $b^{4}$

$$b^{4} = \left(\sqrt{2}_{\,3\pi/4}\right)^{4} = \left(\sqrt{2}^{\,4}\right)_{\,4 \cdot 3\pi/4} = 4_{\,3\pi} = 4_{\,\pi} = -4$$

$3\pi$ is $\pi$ plus one turn. Check: $b^{2} = (-1+i)^{2} = -2i$, and $b^{4} = (-2i)^{2} = -4$.

b.iv) $\dfrac{a^{3}}{b^{2}}$

First the two powers, each with its rule:

$$a^{3} = \left(2_{\pi/3}\right)^{3} = 8_{\pi} \qquad\qquad b^{2} = \left(\sqrt{2}_{\,3\pi/4}\right)^{2} = 2_{\,3\pi/2}$$
$$\dfrac{a^{3}}{b^{2}} = \dfrac{8_{\pi}}{2_{\,3\pi/2}} = 4_{\,\pi - 3\pi/2} = 4_{-\pi/2} = 4_{\,3\pi/2} = -4i$$

Check in Cartesian form: $a^{3} = -8$ and $b^{2} = -2i$, so $\dfrac{-8}{-2i} = \dfrac{4}{i} = -4i$.

b.v) $\dfrac{a^{2}b}{c^{2}}$

$$a^{2} = \left(2_{\pi/3}\right)^{2} = 4_{\,2\pi/3} \qquad\qquad c^{2} = \left(2_{\,11\pi/6}\right)^{2} = 4_{\,11\pi/3}$$

$b$ is in the numerator, so its argument is added; only that of $c^{2}$ is subtracted:

$$\dfrac{a^{2}b}{c^{2}} = \left(\dfrac{4\sqrt{2}}{4}\right)_{\frac{2\pi}{3} + \frac{3\pi}{4} - \frac{11\pi}{3}} = \sqrt{2}_{\,\frac{8\pi + 9\pi - 44\pi}{12}} = \sqrt{2}_{-9\pi/4} = \sqrt{2}_{\,7\pi/4}$$
$$\sqrt{2}_{\,7\pi/4} = \sqrt{2}\left(\cos\dfrac{7\pi}{4} + i\,\sin\dfrac{7\pi}{4}\right) = \sqrt{2}\left(\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2} - \dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\,i\right) = 1 - i$$

Correction to the board

In class the argument of $b$ was subtracted ($\frac{2\pi}{3} - \frac{3\pi}{4} - \frac{11\pi}{3}$) and the answer came out as $\sqrt{2}_{\pi/4} = 1 + i$, the conjugate of the right one. The check in Cartesian form confirms it: $a^{2}b = (2 - 2\sqrt{3}) - (2 + 2\sqrt{3})\,i$ and $c^{2} = 2 - 2\sqrt{3}\,i$, and dividing gives $1 - i$.

Exercise 8F.6

Question

a) Describe the line formed on the Argand plane by all the points $z$ with $\arg z = \frac{\pi}{4}$.

The diagram shows the complex numbers $z = 0.5t\,e^{\frac{\pi}{2}t\,i}$, with $t > 0$, together with the points $z$ for which $\arg z = \theta$ for some angle $\theta$.

b) Find the values of $t$ at which the curve first crosses  i) the imaginary axis  and  ii) the real axis.

c) Give the values of $z$ at these points in Cartesian form.

The curve and the half-line meet at the points A, B and C.

d) Find, in exponential form and in terms of $\theta$, the complex numbers $z_1$, $z_2$ and $z_3$ at the points A, B and C.

e) Write down $|z_2| - |z_1|$ and $|z_3| - |z_2|$. Would you expect this pattern to continue? Justify your answer.

a) The half-line $\arg z = \frac{\pi}{4}$

If $z = x + y\,i$ has argument $\frac{\pi}{4}$, then $\tan\frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{y}{x} = 1$: the real and imaginary parts are equal. But not the whole line $y = x$: the points with $x = y < 0$ have argument $-\frac{3\pi}{4}$, and the origin has no argument.

It is the open half-line $\operatorname{Re}(z) = \operatorname{Im}(z) > 0$: the bisector of the first quadrant, without the origin.

In class it was written as $\operatorname{Re}(z) = \operatorname{Im}(z)$; the condition $> 0$ was missing.

b) First crossings with the axes

$z = 0.5t\,e^{\frac{\pi}{2}t\,i}$ has modulus $0.5t$ and argument $\frac{\pi}{2}t$, that is, $z = 0.5t\cos\frac{\pi t}{2} + i\;0.5t\sin\frac{\pi t}{2}$.

i) On the imaginary axis, $\operatorname{Re}(z) = 0$. Since $t > 0$, the cosine must be zero:

$$\cos\dfrac{\pi t}{2} = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \dfrac{\pi t}{2} = \dfrac{\pi}{2} + k\pi \;\Longrightarrow\; t = 1 + 2k, \quad k \in \mathbb{Z}$$

In class we got the two families $t = 1 + 4k$ and $t = -1 + 4k$: together they are the odd numbers, $t = 1 + 2k$. With $t > 0$, the first time is $t = 1$.

ii) On the real axis, $\operatorname{Im}(z) = 0$, so $\sin\frac{\pi t}{2} = 0$ and $\frac{\pi t}{2} = k\pi$, that is, $t = 2k$. The first with $t > 0$ is $t = 2$.

i) $t = 1$    ii) $t = 2$

c) The crossing points

$$t = 1:\;\; z = 0.5\,e^{\frac{\pi}{2}i} = 0.5\,i \qquad\qquad t = 2:\;\; z = 1\cdot e^{\pi i} = -1$$

They are the two red points closest to the origin in the diagram.

d) The points A, B and C

The curve meets the half-line when its argument equals $\theta$ plus a whole number of turns:

$$\dfrac{\pi t}{2} = \theta + 2k\pi \;\Longrightarrow\; t = \dfrac{2\theta}{\pi} + 4k \;\Longrightarrow\; |z| = 0.5t = \dfrac{\theta}{\pi} + 2k$$

With $k = 0, 1, 2$:

$z_1 = \dfrac{\theta}{\pi}\,e^{i\theta}, \qquad z_2 = \left(\dfrac{\theta}{\pi} + 2\right)e^{i\theta}, \qquad z_3 = \left(\dfrac{\theta}{\pi} + 4\right)e^{i\theta}$

e) The pattern

$$|z_2| - |z_1| = 2 \qquad\qquad |z_3| - |z_2| = 2$$

Yes, it continues: each full turn the argument grows by $2\pi$, so $t$ grows by $4$ and the modulus $0.5t$ by $2$, whatever $\theta$ is. It is an Archimedean spiral: the turns are always the same distance apart, $2$ units.

Adding two sinusoidal functions

The goal: add two sinusoidal functions with the same frequency (the same $b$) and see that the result is another sinusoid with that frequency, with a new amplitude and phase.

The idea: sine is an imaginary part

For a complex number in exponential form, $r\,e^{i\theta} = r\cos\theta + i\,r\sin\theta$, so

$$\operatorname{Im}\left(r\,e^{i\theta}\right) = r\sin\theta \qquad\qquad \operatorname{Re}\left(r\,e^{i\theta}\right) = r\cos\theta$$

With $\theta = bx + \varphi$, any sinusoid can be written as the imaginary part of a product:

$$A\sin(bx + \varphi) = \operatorname{Im}\left(A\,e^{i(bx + \varphi)}\right) = \operatorname{Im}\left(e^{ibx}\cdot A\,e^{i\varphi}\right)$$

If $f_1(x) = A_1\sin(bx + \varphi_1)$ and $f_2(x) = A_2\sin(bx + \varphi_2)$, the imaginary part of a sum is the sum of the imaginary parts, and $e^{ibx}$ is a common factor because the frequency is the same:

$$f_1(x) + f_2(x) = \operatorname{Im}\left(e^{ibx}\left(A_1e^{i\varphi_1} + A_2e^{i\varphi_2}\right)\right)$$

The result

The bracket is a fixed complex number that does not depend on $x$. Writing it as $R\,e^{i\alpha}$:

$$f_1(x) + f_2(x) = \operatorname{Im}\left(R\,e^{i(bx + \alpha)}\right) = R\sin(bx + \alpha)$$
$$R = \left|A_1e^{i\varphi_1} + A_2e^{i\varphi_2}\right| \qquad \alpha = \arg\left(A_1e^{i\varphi_1} + A_2e^{i\varphi_2}\right)$$

In practice, the bracket is added on the calculator and the modulus and argument are read off.

Watch the calculator mode: if the phases are in degrees, degrees; if they are in radians, radians. And to check the modulus by hand, use the cosine rule: $R^{2} = A_1^{2} + A_2^{2} + 2A_1A_2\cos(\varphi_1 - \varphi_2)$.

Example 1 · $\sin x + 2\sin\left(x + \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$

Let $f(x) = \sin x$ and $g(x) = 2\sin\left(x + \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$, and $h(x) = f(x) + g(x)$.

Each one as an imaginary part

$$f(x) = \operatorname{Im}\left(e^{ix}\right) \qquad g(x) = \operatorname{Im}\left(2\,e^{i\left(x + \frac{\pi}{2}\right)}\right) = \operatorname{Im}\left(2\,e^{ix}\,e^{\frac{\pi}{2}i}\right)$$
$$h(x) = \operatorname{Im}\left(e^{ix} + 2\,e^{ix}\,e^{\frac{\pi}{2}i}\right) = \operatorname{Im}\left(e^{ix}\left(1 + 2\,e^{\frac{\pi}{2}i}\right)\right)$$

The bracket in exponential form

$$1 + 2\,e^{\frac{\pi}{2}i} = 1 + 2i, \qquad |1 + 2i| = \sqrt{5} \approx 2.24, \qquad \arg(1 + 2i) = \arctan 2 \approx 1.107$$
$$h(x) = \operatorname{Im}\left(\sqrt{5}\,e^{i(x + 1.107)}\right) = \sqrt{5}\,\sin(x + 1.107)$$
$h(x) \approx 2.24\,\sin(x + 1.11)$

Plotting $\sin x + 2\sin\left(x + \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$ and $2.24\sin(x + 1.11)$ on the calculator, the two graphs coincide. The amplitude of the sum is $\sqrt{5} \approx 2.24$, not $1 + 2 = 3$: the two waves are not in phase, and the amplitudes would only add up if they had the same phase.

Example 2 · Find $r$ and $\alpha$

If $f(x) = 3\sin\left(3x + \frac{\pi}{12}\right)$ and $g(x) = 4\sin\left(3x + \frac{3\pi}{4}\right)$, find $r$ and $\alpha$ such that $f(x) + g(x) = r\sin(3x + \alpha)$.

Solution

$$f(x) + g(x) = \operatorname{Im}\left(e^{3xi}\left(3\,e^{\frac{\pi}{12}i} + 4\,e^{\frac{3\pi}{4}i}\right)\right)$$
$$3\,e^{\frac{\pi}{12}i} + 4\,e^{\frac{3\pi}{4}i} \approx (2.898 + 0.776\,i) + (-2.828 + 2.828\,i) = 0.069 + 3.605\,i$$
$$r = \sqrt{13} \approx 3.61 \qquad \alpha \approx 1.55$$
$f(x) + g(x) = \sqrt{13}\,\sin(3x + 1.55)$

Check with the cosine rule: the phases differ by $\frac{3\pi}{4} - \frac{\pi}{12} = \frac{2\pi}{3}$, and $r^{2} = 9 + 16 + 2\cdot 3\cdot 4\cos\frac{2\pi}{3} = 25 - 12 = 13$.

Homework · Two alternating current sources

Question

Two alternating current sources have voltages of $100$ V and $180$ V. The first has a phase shift of $45^\circ$ and the second of $135^\circ$. The two sources are combined.

a) Find the voltage of the combined source.  b) Find the phase shift of the combined source.

Both sources as imaginary parts

$$V_1 = 100\sin(bt + 45^\circ) = \operatorname{Im}\left(100\,e^{ibt}\,e^{45^\circ i}\right) \qquad V_2 = 180\sin(bt + 135^\circ) = \operatorname{Im}\left(180\,e^{ibt}\,e^{135^\circ i}\right)$$
$$V_T = V_1 + V_2 = \operatorname{Im}\left(e^{ibt}\left(100\,e^{45^\circ i} + 180\,e^{135^\circ i}\right)\right)$$

The bracket, with the calculator in degrees

$$100\,e^{45^\circ i} + 180\,e^{135^\circ i} \approx (70.71 + 70.71\,i) + (-127.28 + 127.28\,i) = -56.57 + 197.99\,i$$

It is in the second quadrant. Modulus and argument:

$$R = \sqrt{56.57^{2} + 197.99^{2}} \approx 205.9 \qquad \alpha = 180^\circ - \arctan\dfrac{197.99}{56.57} \approx 180^\circ - 74.1^\circ = 105.9^\circ$$
$V_T \approx 206\,\sin(bt + 105.9^\circ)$:   a) $206$ V    b) $105.9^\circ$

A quick check: the phases differ by $135^\circ - 45^\circ = 90^\circ$, so the two vectors are perpendicular and the amplitude follows from Pythagoras, $\sqrt{100^{2} + 180^{2}} = \sqrt{42\,400} \approx 205.9$.

Correction to the board

In class we got $V_T = 162.08\sin(bt + 141.45^\circ)$. That is what the calculator gives in radian mode: it reads $45$ and $135$ as radians, not degrees. With phase shifts in degrees, the calculator must be in degrees. The right answer is $206$ V and $105.9^\circ$.
