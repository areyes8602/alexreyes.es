Complex numbers: $i$ and Cartesian form

Where the imaginary unit comes from, how to work in Cartesian form, and what each complex number means as a point of the plane.

The problem that makes them necessary

Take any quadratic equation:

$$x^2 + x + 3 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = \dfrac{-1 \pm \sqrt{1 - 4\cdot 1 \cdot 3}}{2\cdot 1} = \dfrac{-1 \pm \sqrt{-11}}{2}$$

The discriminant is negative, so it has no real solution: no real number squared gives $-11$. The equation is neither odd nor badly posed; the reals simply fall short.

The imaginary unit

Define $i$ as the number satisfying

$$i = \sqrt{-1} \qquad\qquad i^2 = -1$$

With $i$, the root of a negative splits: $\sqrt{-11} = \sqrt{11}\cdot\sqrt{-1} = \sqrt{11}\,i$. And then the equation does have two solutions:

$$x = \dfrac{-1 \pm \sqrt{11}\,i}{2} \;\Longrightarrow\; x_1 = \dfrac{-1 + \sqrt{11}\,i}{2}, \qquad x_2 = \dfrac{-1 - \sqrt{11}\,i}{2}$$

The two solutions differ only in the sign of the imaginary part. That always happens when the coefficients are real: complex roots come in conjugate pairs.

The powers of $i$

They all follow from $i^2=-1$:

$$i^2 = -1 \qquad i^3 = i^2\cdot i = -i \qquad i^4 = 1 \qquad i^5 = i^4 \cdot i = i$$

The cycle repeats every four

Since $i^4=1$, raising $i$ to a large exponent only needs the remainder of the exponent divided by 4. For instance $i^{39}$: since $39 = 4\cdot 9 + 3$, we get $i^{39} = i^3 = -i$.

Cartesian form

Definition

Every complex number is written

$$z = a + b\,i$$

→ $a$ is the real part, $\operatorname{Re}(z)$.

→ $b$ is the imaginary part, $\operatorname{Im}(z)$.

Both are real numbers: the imaginary part is $b$, not $bi$.

If $b=0$ what is left is a real number, so the reals are the complex numbers with zero imaginary part: $\mathbb{R}$ sits inside $\mathbb{C}$. If $a=0$ the number is called purely imaginary.

Working in Cartesian form

You work as with any binomial, replacing $i^2$ by $-1$ at the end:

$$(3-2i) + (-1+2i) = 2 + 0i = 2$$
$$(3-2i)\cdot(-1+2i) = -3 + 6i + 2i - 4i^2 = -3 + 8i + 4 = 1 + 8i$$

The Argand diagram

A complex number holds two numbers, so it does not fit on a line: it is drawn as a point of the plane. Real part on the horizontal axis, imaginary part on the vertical one.

The plane used this way is called the complex plane, and the picture an Argand diagram. The horizontal axis is the ordinary real line.

Conjugate, modulus and argument

Plotting on the plane gives three quantities that can be read straight off the picture, and they are the ones used later.

Conjugate

$z^{*} = a - b\,i$: the same point reflected in the real axis. Only the sign of the imaginary part changes.

Modulus

$|z| = \sqrt{a^2+b^2}$: the distance from the point to the origin. It is Pythagoras on the two legs $a$ and $b$.

Argument

$\arg(z)$: the angle it makes with the positive real axis, measured anticlockwise.

Example

For $z_1 = 3-2i$:

$$z_1^{*} = 3+2i \qquad |z_1| = \sqrt{3^2+(-2)^2} = \sqrt{13} \approx 3{,}61$$
$$\arg(z_1) = \arctan\!\left(\dfrac{-2}{3}\right) = -0{,}588 \ \text{rad}$$

It comes out negative because the point lies below the real axis, in the fourth quadrant.

Careful with the arctangent

The calculator always returns an angle between $-\frac{\pi}{2}$ and $\frac{\pi}{2}$, that is in the first or fourth quadrant. If the point lies to the left ($a<0$), you must add $\pi$ to the result. Check the diagram before accepting the argument: the picture says which quadrant it must land in.

An example of the tricky case

For $z_2 = -1+2i$, which lies in the second quadrant:

$$\arctan\!\left(\dfrac{2}{-1}\right) = -1{,}107 \quad \text{(fourth quadrant: not it)}$$
$$\arg(z_2) = -1{,}107 + \pi = 2{,}034 \ \text{rad}$$

Modulus and argument are what let you write the complex number in polar and exponential form, which comes next and is what makes complex numbers useful for waves.
