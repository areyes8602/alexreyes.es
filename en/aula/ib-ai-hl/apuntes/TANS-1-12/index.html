The imaginary unit

The problem that forces us to invent complex numbers, and the powers of $i$.

The problem that makes them necessary

Take any quadratic equation:

$$x^2 + x + 3 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = \dfrac{-1 \pm \sqrt{1 - 4\cdot 1 \cdot 3}}{2\cdot 1} = \dfrac{-1 \pm \sqrt{-11}}{2}$$

The discriminant is negative, so it has no real solution: no real number squared gives $-11$. The equation is neither odd nor badly posed; the reals simply fall short.

The imaginary unit

Define $i$ as the number satisfying

$$i = \sqrt{-1} \qquad\qquad i^2 = -1$$

With $i$, the root of a negative splits: $\sqrt{-11} = \sqrt{11}\cdot\sqrt{-1} = \sqrt{11}\,i$. And then the equation does have two solutions:

$$x = \dfrac{-1 \pm \sqrt{11}\,i}{2} \;\Longrightarrow\; x_1 = \dfrac{-1 + \sqrt{11}\,i}{2}, \qquad x_2 = \dfrac{-1 - \sqrt{11}\,i}{2}$$

The two solutions differ only in the sign of the imaginary part. That always happens when the coefficients are real: complex roots come in conjugate pairs.

The powers of $i$

They all follow from $i^2=-1$:

$$i^2 = -1 \qquad i^3 = i^2\cdot i = -i \qquad i^4 = 1 \qquad i^5 = i^4 \cdot i = i$$

The cycle repeats every four

Since $i^4=1$, raising $i$ to a large exponent only needs the remainder of the exponent divided by 4. For instance $i^{39}$: since $39 = 4\cdot 9 + 3$, we get $i^{39} = i^3 = -i$.
